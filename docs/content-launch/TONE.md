# Trilorah voice for product writing

Use this guide for Trilorah articles, feature introductions and proposed interface copy. The primary reader is a church volunteer preparing or running a service. The founder has asked us to proceed with the rebuild using bold, cheeky humour delivered with a serious face, including short ad-libs. Individual new jokes remain editorial choices, not separately approved quotations.

## The person behind the words

Trilorah knows its way around the presentation desk and has an opinion about unnecessary work. It explains things plainly. Occasionally it takes slightly too much credit for helping. The confidence belongs in the observation and the demonstrated feature; it does not require calling the product revolutionary.

Use “we” when the brand is speaking and “you” for the reader's action. A clearly identified in-app companion may use “I”. Do not switch an article into an unexplained mascot voice halfway through.

## A technology brand speaking as “we”

Trilorah speaks as the team building the technology. Use “we” to own our work, decisions and point of view: “we build”, “we designed” or “we’re working on”. Name Trilorah or the relevant feature when explaining what the software does. Keep intentions, current capabilities and future work distinct.

The voice should sound professional, direct and comfortable with having an opinion. Use ordinary words and natural contractions. Show the intelligence through a useful behaviour the reader can recognise. Keep the dry humour and short asides where they earn their place; a headline or supporting sentence can carry the voice without a joke.

For homepage supporting copy, connect what we build to the people and work it serves. Explain one useful way the technology helps in one or two sentences. Leave the full feature inventory to the product sections. The Christian purpose belongs in the real church context and the work the software supports.

## What the founder liked and rejected

“Your phone has been promoted” is the preferred starting direction. The founder suggested parenthetical additions such as “you should thank me”. The useful device is a feature-specific setup followed by a small extra beat of attitude.

“Sunday has declined to move” and “The preacher has taken a detour” were rejected as AI-like attempts at humour. Do not replace them with another vaguely personified calendar, desk, booth or app setting. The full feedback record is in [voice calibration](voice-calibration.md).

Examples of the current direction:

- Mobile feature: “Your phone has joined the media team. (It was here every Sunday anyway.)”
- Mobile promotion: “Finally, a reason to be on your phone in church. (An official one.)”
- Companion locating a requested control: “Here's the button. (I do work here.)”

These show a pattern, not mandatory lines or permission to repeat the same joke everywhere.

## How to write a line with personality

Start from something the reader recognises: using a phone in church, receiving slides late, preparing work for another volunteer, or checking what the back row can read. Give the sentence a clear useful meaning. Then see whether an admission, a small boast or a second interpretation adds anything.

A parenthetical aside should change the beat. “You're welcome” attached to every benefit becomes a habit, not a voice. “Literally” belongs only where a real second meaning supports it. Keep the aside short enough to say in one breath. Do not add a laugh, explain the punchline or follow it with three more jokes.

Most paragraphs need no joke. The reader came to understand or do something. Humour can open a story, reward a completed task or acknowledge a familiar hassle; precise directions carry the rest.

## Match the moment

| Moment | How it should sound | What to keep clear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product or feature introduction | Direct, confident, occasionally cheeky | Who it helps, what it does and the conditions for using it |
| A product decision or founder story | Personal, specific and willing to have an opinion | The real reason and evidence; do not invent the founder's history |
| A beginner guide | Friendly and practical; optional light opening | Exact control names, ordered steps and a visible result |
| Live-service troubleshooting | Calm and literal | The next check, recovery action and consequences |
| Confirmation or a completed guide | Brief; a small aside can fit | That the action actually succeeded |
| Release notes | Concrete and compact | What changed, the relevant version, affected users and next action |
| A limitation | Straightforward | What will not work in that setup and the available alternative |

Keep **Go live**, **Save**, pairing consent and other consequential controls understandable. A playful subtitle can sit beside a literal action; it must not hide what pressing the button does.

## Words and habits to remove

Cut empty claims such as “seamless”, “game-changing”, “revolutionary”, “effortlessly”, “unlock”, “elevate”, “supercharge”, “magic” and “the future of worship”. Use a concrete action or result instead. Do not describe the reader's church as deficient to make the product impressive.

Avoid generic hooks about a fast-paced world, rhetorical questions answered immediately by the writer, contrived personification, forced internet slang, repeated three-item slogans, and “not X, but Y” constructions. Read a paragraph aloud: if it sounds like an advert trying to make friends, rewrite it as something a person at the desk would actually say.

Use contractions naturally. Let sentence length vary. Short sentences work when they carry a thought; a whole page of fragments feels manufactured. Technical words belong where the reader needs them, with a brief explanation on first use.

## Claims must survive a demonstration

Every product statement needs current source evidence and the relevant release check. Implemented code is not proof of availability in a shipped build. A story drafted ahead of release can be complete while remaining held for verification.

In particular, mobile controls require the connected desktop and the supported network setup; newly caught scripture suggestions require an explicit live action, while separately enabled Auto advance can follow reading through scripture already live; the companion covers supported workflows; programme import still needs review; a correction is not a universal accuracy guarantee; and a service package does not configure another computer's hardware. Recheck these conditions when behaviour changes. The [product context](../../.agents/product-marketing.md) points to current evidence for these distinctions.

Keep internal evidence and production notes outside reader copy. Keep conditions the user needs inside the explanation, near the relevant claim. Do not invent customer quotations, time saved, recognition percentages, launch dates or founder motives.

## Choose a component for a reason

| Reader needs to… | Use… | Avoid… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a feature's purpose | A short concrete scenario followed by the explanation | A long fictional customer story |
| Find a control | A cropped, annotated screenshot | An unreadable full-app screenshot for one small button |
| See something change | A short captioned recording and a written equivalent | A static picture claiming to prove motion or recognition |
| Complete a sequence | Numbered steps | Burying actions inside a story |
| Check readiness | A checklist | A decorative checklist repeating the introduction |
| Compare options | A small table using the same criteria | A table made from unrelated marketing claims |
| Understand connections | A labelled diagram or simple map | Decorative arrows or invented architecture |
| Diagnose a problem | Observable checks and a short decision tree | A joke before the recovery action |
| Follow up or verify | A descriptive guide link or source beside its claim | An unexplained list of references at the end |

There is no mandatory number of screenshots, lists or diagrams per article. Choose the minimum that makes the actual task clearer. Use original rehearsal material and real UI; a generated product screenshot is not evidence.

## Editing passes

First check the story's purpose and order. Next remove unclear or padded sentences. Then verify every capability and condition. Read aloud for rhythm and cut any joke that needs explanation. Finally check the headline, links, exact UI labels, visual captions and publication status.

Before publishing a new voice pattern broadly, let the founder react to a small set of examples and try the winning approach in a second context. Retain accepted and rejected examples with the actual feedback. Do not treat silence as a positive response or assume every reader shares the same cultural references.

This guide records reusable writing context. It does not retrain a model or install an agent skill. It is the shared reference for this content library's next draft and review.
