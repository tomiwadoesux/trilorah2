# Brand writing study: five voice exemplars

Research date: **11 October 2026**. Audience: church volunteers setting up and running services with Trilorah.

These are selected examples of useful writing techniques, not an objective ranking of the world's best copywriters. The observations below come from current official pages. Interpretations and Trilorah examples are editorial recommendations, not claims made by those brands or evidence that a technique improves conversion.

**Voice is awaiting approval.** The user first chose bold, opinionated and a little cheeky, then clarified: Duolingo-inspired mischief delivered with a completely serious face. This document records research; it does not commit Trilorah to a finished voice. Transfer examples are original exploration, not approved website copy or announcements of feature availability.

## 1. Apple: one benefit, then the evidence

**Read:** [AirPods Pro 3](https://www.apple.com/airpods-pro/) and [Connect your AirPods and AirPods Pro to your iPhone](https://support.apple.com/en-us/104989), accessed 2026-10-11.

**Observed:** The product page moves from a prominent benefit into brief feature sections, images, concrete use situations, comparisons and qualification notes. Headings sometimes carry wordplay; supporting sentences explain the actual behaviour. The support article changes register: a literal task title, prerequisites, numbered actions, a setup image and a fallback route when pairing does not work.

**Interpretation:** The hierarchy does much of the writing. A reader can skim the promise, inspect its demonstration, then choose the details. Marketing and help share a product but have different jobs.

**Borrow for Trilorah:** Give a feature introduction one clear promise. Follow it with a real interface capture and a specific action. Put requirements close to the claim they qualify. Link to the full setup guide.

**Avoid:** Unsupported superlatives, multiplying adjectives, or borrowing Apple's habit of describing technology as magic. Volunteers need to know what will happen on the church screen.

**Original transfer example:** “Find the verse while the sermon keeps moving.” Follow with a short recording of a spoken reference becoming a suggestion, then explain the operator's review and projection controls.

## 2. Mailchimp: personality that knows when to stop

**Read:** [Voice and Tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/) and [How to Write Educational Content](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/writing-educational-content/), accessed 2026-10-11.

**Observed:** Mailchimp explicitly distinguishes a relatively stable voice from tone that responds to the reader's situation. Its guidance prioritises clarity, subtle humour and avoiding condescension. Its education guide recommends staying on the reader's question, using descriptive headings, matching interface labels, and tightly cropping visuals around the relevant action. Related topics become links rather than digressions.

**Interpretation:** Sounding human is partly emotional accuracy. A witty line can fit an introduction and fail inside a troubleshooting instruction.

**Borrow for Trilorah:** Keep the same helpful person behind the copy, with less mischief as urgency increases. Announcements can have a dry aside; microphone troubleshooting should identify the cause, the next action and the expected result. Bold exact UI labels in instructions.

**Avoid:** Jokes that delay an answer, repeated reassurance, invented slang and treating a new volunteer's confusion as funny.

**Original transfer example:** “The microphone has one job. Let's check it's doing it.” Appropriate as a light guide opening; the next sentence must give a concrete check, without another joke.

## 3. Basecamp: make a claim you are prepared to explain

**Read:** [Basecamp](https://basecamp.com/) and [Basecamp 5 is all new for 2026](https://basecamp.com/5), accessed 2026-10-11.

**Observed:** The homepage names the work customers manage, includes a direct founder letter, demonstrates the interface and offers a short product tour. The release page groups changes under descriptive feature headings. It explains what an old interaction required and what someone can now do, with an accompanying image. Short conversational judgments punctuate longer explanations. Named customer comments appear throughout.

**Interpretation:** The confidence comes from choosing a position and showing a mechanism. The writer sounds invested because specific inconveniences are named.

**Borrow for Trilorah:** Introduce a feature through the Sunday problem it removes. Explain the changed interaction. Show it. Finish with a useful next action. Use customer testimony only when it is real, attributable and approved.

**Avoid:** Copying broad attacks on other software, empty victory language, or inventing a before-and-after result before testing it.

**Original transfer example:** “Your service plan should survive a change of laptop.” Use this as a proposed story angle for service export and import; verify precisely which assets travel with the file before publishing.

## 4. Monzo: warmth lives in specifics

**Read:** [Monzo Writing Principles](https://monzo.com/tone-of-voice) and [Monzo Pots](https://monzo.com/features/pots), accessed 2026-10-11.

**Observed:** The principles put reader impact ahead of internal reasoning and distinguish everyday clarity from marketing wit. They recommend reading aloud and being careful with expressions that may exclude readers. The Pots page explains the feature through familiar spending situations, pairs individual benefits with app images, gives a short setup sequence and answers concrete questions about limitations and behaviour.

**Interpretation:** Specific situations make the product feel friendly without announcing that it is friendly. The FAQ earns its space by answering questions a person may actually have.

**Borrow for Trilorah:** Write about the replacement volunteer, the late announcement and the unfamiliar laptop. Keep the mechanism plain. Use FAQs for real uncertainty: who controls the output, what a phone needs to connect, what happens if connectivity drops.

**Avoid:** Importing financial metaphors, niche cultural references or repeating a call to action after every small paragraph.

**Original transfer example:** “Someone else is running the slides this Sunday. Leave them a service plan, not a scavenger hunt.” A proposed volunteer-handover angle; follow immediately with the practical preparation steps.

## 5. Oatly: the writer is visibly in the room

**Read:** [Oatly who?](https://www.oatly.com/en-us/oatly-who) and [Oatmilk Barista Edition](https://www.oatly.com/en-us/products/oatmilk/oatmilk-barista-edition-32-oz), accessed 2026-10-11.

**Observed:** The company page acknowledges its own mission statement and circles back to it. The product page directly addresses both specialists and ordinary users, interrupts itself, and mixes practical explanations with self-aware asides. It also separates product advantages, ingredient concerns, practical uses, questions and a factual nutrition table.

**Interpretation:** The visible narrator makes the prose distinctive. Its conversational detours suit a brand visit more than a time-sensitive support task. The useful structural lesson is that personality and factual detail can occupy different components.

**Borrow for Trilorah:** A brief straight-faced aside can acknowledge a familiar inconvenience. Let the product explanation remain clear. Answer awkward limitations openly in a small, accurately titled section.

**Avoid:** Long rambles, deliberately messy spelling, provocation for its own sake, or making church beliefs and volunteers the joke. Never obscure a consequential control with a clever label.

**Original transfer example:** “Five minutes before the service is an ambitious time to discover a new button.” A proposed opening for a rehearsal guide; continue with a short checklist that actually helps.

## Component choices for Trilorah

The choices below are recommendations from the study, not a claim that every examined brand uses every component.

| Reader's need | Component | Trilorah use |
|---|---|---|
| Understand why a feature matters | Short story followed by a product demonstration | A volunteer hears a verse reference and checks the suggestion |
| Find a control | Cropped screenshot with one annotation | Identify the input selector without showing the entire app |
| See an interaction or sequence | Short captioned clip | Watch a companion demonstration, a preview change or a remote action |
| Complete a task in order | Numbered steps | Connect an input, confirm signal, check recognition |
| Check readiness | Checklist | Before-service audio, output and slide checks |
| Choose between genuinely different options | Comparison table | Local versus remote operation, using only tested distinctions |
| Understand connections or ownership | Small labelled diagram | Phone, presentation computer, connection and audience output; include only verified paths |
| Resolve a conditional failure | Decision tree | Signal present or absent, then the next diagnostic check |
| Understand a limitation | Adjacent note or short limitations section | Supported setup, access requirements, operator control |
| Follow a deeper explanation | Descriptive link | A feature story links to its setup guide |
| Assess an externally sourced claim | Source beside the claim | Third-party compatibility, a quoted customer result or research |

Do not turn every article into a component catalogue. Choose the smallest set that makes this particular subject easier to understand. Decorative diagrams and generic stock imagery do not count as evidence.

## What would make future drafts better

1. Keep a source record for each feature: shipped behaviour, interface labels, prerequisites, limitations and what still needs testing.
2. Collect the real phrases volunteers use when asking for help, with appropriate permission. Keep them separate from invented scenarios.
3. Ask the founder to approve a small set of example lines across announcements, guides and error messages before rewriting the library.
4. Build a small approved example bank. Record why a line works; a banned-word list alone cannot teach judgment.
5. Write the factual explanation first, then add one purposeful moment of personality. Remove it if the sentence becomes less clear.
6. Read the draft aloud. Vary sentence length. Remove throat-clearing, predictable three-part lists and enthusiasm that says nothing specific.
7. Check the article against the current app before publishing. A source-code implementation is not proof that every real-world setup works.
8. Test guides by asking a new volunteer to complete the task. Record where they hesitate and revise those parts.

Mobile controls and other future differentiators should have release-gated drafts: an original story and structure can be prepared now, while exact capabilities, screenshots, requirements and publication timing remain tied to verified releases.
